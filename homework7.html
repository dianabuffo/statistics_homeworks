Modes of Convergence and Bayesian Inference

The four ways in which a random sequence can "converge" and what each one means for the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem (and why √n appears), then conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and the prior–likelihood–posterior machinery of Bayesian inference.

pt1 pt2
1. Four meanings of "converges"
Almost sure, in probability, in mean square and in distribution: definitions, the implications between them, and counterexamples that show why they differ.
2. LLN, CLT and the √n
Which mode each theorem uses, and why the standard deviation of a sum grows like √n when observations are independent (and what breaks when they are not).
3. From Bayes' theorem to inference
Prior, likelihood and posterior; sequential updating; examples with Beta–Binomial and Normal–Normal; advantages, limits and the influence of the prior.

What are we doing and what is the objective?

pt1: a statement like "Xn tends to X" has several non-equivalent meanings for random variables. We define the four main ones, show how they are related (and with counterexamples that they are not equivalent), identify which of them is used by the Law of Large Numbers and by the Central Limit Theorem, and explain why the factor √n shows up in these theorems.

pt2: we recall conditional probability and Bayes' theorem, introduce the three components of Bayesian inference (prior, likelihood, posterior), see how Bayes' theorem turns data into updated beliefs, work out examples and discuss advantages, limitations and the influence of the prior on the conclusions.

The objective: to be able to say exactly what a convergence theorem claims (and what it does not), and to read a Bayesian analysis as a precise, step-by-step transformation of a prior distribution into a posterior distribution through the likelihood.

Study guide: start here (the vocabulary, from zero)

For pt1 (convergence)

  • Random variable X(ω): a number determined by the outcome ω of a random experiment. A sequence X1, X2, … is a list of such random variables, all defined on the same experiment.
  • One "run" of the experiment is a single ω: it produces a whole numeric sequence X1(ω), X2(ω), … that we can look at as an ordinary sequence of numbers. Convergence modes differ in how they use these runs.
  • i.i.d.: independent and identically distributed. Sample mean: X̄n = (X1 + … + Xn)/n. Estimator: a recipe that turns data into a guess of an unknown parameter. Consistent estimator: it converges to the true value as n grows.
  • Event with probability 1 ("almost surely"): it can fail only on a set of outcomes of probability 0.
  • CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x): the distribution function. A continuity point is an x where F has no jump.

For pt2 (Bayes)

  • Conditional probability P(A | B): the probability of A when we know that B happened.
  • Parameter θ: an unknown quantity of the model (for example the success probability of an attack). Data x: what we observed.
  • Prior π(θ): what we believe about θ before seeing the data. Likelihood L(θ; x) = p(x | θ): how probable the observed data are for each possible value of θ. Posterior π(θ | x): what we believe about θ after seeing the data. Evidence p(x): the normalizing constant.
  • Odds: p/(1 − p). Likelihood ratio (Bayes factor): how many times more probable the data are under one hypothesis than under another.
  • Credible interval: an interval that contains θ with, say, 95% posterior probability; different from a frequentist confidence interval.
How to study this page. In pt1 start with the "sample mean" example in the counterexample lab (everything converges), then switch to each counterexample and watch which curve fails to go down. In pt2 play with the sliders until you can predict, before moving them, what will happen to the posterior.

Modes of convergence in probability theory

Differences between the main modes of convergence, their role in the LLN and the CLT, and the reason for the √n.

Explain the differences between the main modes of convergence used in probability theory and discuss their respective roles in the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT): convergence in probability, almost sure convergence, mean-square (L²) convergence, convergence in distribution. Discuss why the term √n frequently appears in convergence theorems, in relation to the additivity of variance under suitable conditions and the role of independence, both in probability theory and in statistical inference.

1 The four modes of convergence

Let X1, X2, … and X be random variables on the same probability space. What does "Xn → X" mean?

Mode Definition In words
Almost sure (a.s.)
Xn → X a.s.		 P( limn→∞ Xn = X ) = 1
equivalent: for every ε > 0, P( supk≥n |Xk − X| > ε ) → 0		 Pathwise. For (almost) every run ω the ordinary numeric sequence Xn(ω) converges to X(ω). After some (random) time the run stays within ε for ever.
In probability
Xn →P X		 for every ε > 0: P( |Xn − X| > ε ) → 0 At a fixed large n the chance of being farther than ε from X is small. The runs that are "bad" may be different at each n, and each run may be bad infinitely often.
Mean square (L²)
Xn →L² X		 E[ (Xn − X)² ] → 0 Average squared error tends to zero. It looks at the whole distribution of the error, including rare but huge values. (Generalization: Lp, E|Xn − X|p → 0.)
In distribution
Xn →d X		 Fn(x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of F
equivalent: E g(Xn) → E g(X) for every bounded continuous g		 The law converges, not the variables. It only says that the histogram of Xn looks more and more like that of X. Xn and X need not even be defined on the same experiment.

How they are related

Summary. a.s. ⇒ in probability; L² ⇒ in probability; in probability ⇒ in distribution. None of the arrows can be reversed in general, and a.s. and L² are not comparable. Extra facts: (i) in probability + domination (|Xn| ≤ Y with E Y² < ∞) ⇒ L²; (ii) if ∑n P(|Xn − X| > ε) < ∞ for every ε (Borel–Cantelli lemma) then a.s.; (iii) in probability ⇒ a.s. along a subsequence; (iv) in distribution to a constant ⇒ in probability.

3 Roles in the LLN, the CLT and other results

Result Statement (Xi i.i.d., mean μ, variance σ²) Mode What it adds
Weak LLN (Chebyshev, Khinchin) X̄n → μ in probability Chebyshev: P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). Khinchin: only E|X| < ∞ is needed.
Mean-square version E(X̄n − μ)² = σ²/n → 0 L² It is the MSE of X̄n as an estimator of μ, and it implies the weak LLN.
Strong LLN (Kolmogorov) X̄n → μ almost surely Holds iff E|X| < ∞. It says that the single sequence of averages of one experiment converges: the justification of "long-run frequency".
CLT (Lindeberg–Lévy) Zn = √n (X̄n − μ)/σ → N(0, 1) in distribution The fluctuations of X̄n around μ are of order σ/√n and Gaussian in shape. Rate: Berry–Esseen, supx|Fn(x) − Φ(x)| ≤ Cρ/(σ³√n).
Continuous mapping, Slutsky if Xn →d X and Yn →P c then Xn + Yn →d X + c, XnYn →d cX mix of both Allows replacing the unknown σ by the estimate s: √n(X̄n − μ)/s → N(0, 1). The t-statistic, confidence intervals and tests rely on it.
Delta method √n(g(X̄n) − g(μ)) →d N(0, g′(μ)²σ²) distribution Gives the asymptotic variance of functions of estimates (rates, ratios, logarithms).
Consistency of estimators θ̂n → θ in probability (or a.s.) A minimum requirement for an estimator. Sufficient: bias → 0 and variance → 0, i.e. MSE → 0 (L²).
Asymptotic normality of the MLE √n(θ̂n − θ) →d N(0, 1/I(θ)) distribution The basis for standard errors and Wald tests. I(θ) is the Fisher information per observation.
Glivenko–Cantelli supx|F̂n(x) − F(x)| → 0 almost surely The empirical CDF converges uniformly to the true one: the foundation of non-parametric statistics and of the bootstrap.
Why the CLT cannot be stated in a stronger mode. The standardized sums Zn do not converge in probability or almost surely to anything: Zn and Z2n have correlation 1/√2, and by the law of the iterated logarithm limsup Zn/√(2 ln ln n) = 1 a.s., so along each run the Zn keep wandering without settling. Only their law converges. Meanwhile X̄n itself does converge a.s. to μ: the LLN describes where the average goes, the CLT how it fluctuates around it at the scale 1/√n.

4 Why √n? Additivity of variance and the role of independence

The variance of a sum Var(X1 + … + Xn) = ∑i Var(Xi) + 2 ∑i<j Cov(Xi, Xj)

If the variables are independent (it is enough that they are uncorrelated) every covariance vanishes and, with common variance σ², Var(Sn) = nσ²: variances add. Since the standard deviation is the square root of the variance, SD(Sn) = σ√n and SD(X̄n) = σ/√n.

That is where the √n comes from
  • The only scaling that stabilizes the fluctuations. Var(Sn/na) = σ² n1 − 2a: it explodes for a < 1/2, collapses to 0 for a > 1/2 and stays equal to σ² only for a = 1/2. Dividing by √n is therefore the unique normalization that gives a non-degenerate limit: the CLT.
  • Cancellation. Positive and negative deviations partly cancel; the sum of n independent deviations is typically of size √n, not n.
  • In statistical inference: standard error SE(X̄) = s/√n; confidence interval X̄ ± z s/√n; test statistic √n(X̄ − μ0)/s; sample size n ≈ (zσ/E)² for a margin of error E; MLE: √n(θ̂ − θ) → N(0, 1/I(θ)): estimators are "√n-consistent". Four times more data halve the error; a hundred times more give one more digit.
What happens without independence

Let σ² = 1 and ρ the correlation. Two common cases:

AR(1) (weak dependence, Cov(Xi, Xi+h) = ρh): Var(Sn) = n(1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ) − 2ρ(1 − ρn)/(1 − ρ)² ∼ n · (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)
Equicorrelated (all pairs have correlation ρ): Var(Sn) = n + n(n − 1)ρ ∼ ρ n²
  • Weak dependence (short memory): still Var(Sn) ∼ nσ²LR with a long-run variance σ²LR = σ²(1 + 2∑h≥1 ρ(h)). The √n law and a CLT survive, but with a different constant: the effective sample size is n/(1 + 2∑ρ(h)).
  • Strong dependence: if correlations do not decay, Var(Sn) grows like n². Then dividing by √n does not stabilize anything, and even the LLN fails: X̄n does not tend to μ but to a random limit (the common component).

5 Conclusions of pt1

1. The four modes

Almost sure convergence is pathwise (each run converges); in probability controls the chance of a deviation at a fixed n; mean square controls the average squared deviation; in distribution only controls the shape of the law. The implications a.s. ⇒ P, L² ⇒ P and P ⇒ d hold, and counterexamples show that no reverse holds (typewriter, spike, (−1)nZ), except that distribution to a constant is the same as probability.

2. LLN and CLT

The weak LLN uses convergence in probability (and its usual proof is via L²), the strong LLN uses a.s. convergence, the CLT uses convergence in distribution only: the standardized sums never settle down, but their law approaches N(0, 1). In statistics: consistency (probability), MSE (L²), asymptotic normality and confidence intervals (distribution), Slutsky to combine them.

3. The √n

For independent (or uncorrelated) variables the variances add, Var(Sn) = nσ², so the typical size of the sum is σ√n and that of the mean σ/√n; √n is the only scaling giving a non-trivial limit. With weak dependence the law remains with a different constant (long-run variance); with strong dependence it breaks down (variance ∝ n²) and the LLN itself can fail. In inference, √n sets the standard error, the sample size needed for a given precision and the rate at which estimators converge.

6 Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

Conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) for P(B) > 0

Knowing that B occurred, we restrict the sample space to B and measure A inside it. Consequences: the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B); the law of total probability for a partition B1, …, Bk: P(A) = ∑i P(A | Bi)P(Bi); and independence: A and B are independent iff P(A | B) = P(A).

In statistics

The same idea is applied to relative frequencies: the conditional frequency nij/ni· of Homework 6 is the proportion of F = j within the subgroup X = i. For random variables we have conditional distributions p(y | x) and conditional expectations E[Y | X = x], the best predictor of Y given X in the mean-square sense. In parametric inference the model itself is a conditional distribution: p(data | θ).

Bayes' theorem

Write P(A ∩ B) in two ways, P(A | B)P(B) = P(B | A)P(A), and solve for P(A | B):

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B) with P(B) = ∑i P(B | Ai)P(Ai) (total probability)

Odds form (for two hypotheses H and its complement):

P(H | data)/P(¬H | data) = [ P(H)/P(¬H) ] × [ P(data | H)/P(data | ¬H) ] posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio

Example (spam filter). 40% of the emails are spam; the word "free" appears in 30% of spam and in 2% of legitimate email. P(spam | "free") = 0.4·0.30 / (0.4·0.30 + 0.6·0.02) = 0.12/0.132 = 0.909. The prior 40% became 91% after the evidence; the likelihood ratio was 0.30/0.02 = 15.

8 Prior, likelihood, posterior: the three components

Bayes' theorem for a parameter θ given data x: π(θ | x) = L(θ; x) · π(θ) / p(x) p(x) = ∫ L(θ; x) π(θ) dθ posterior ∝ likelihood × prior
Component Symbol What it is Where it comes from
Prior π(θ) A probability distribution over the possible values of the parameter before seeing the data. Previous studies, expert knowledge, physical constraints, or a deliberately vague "reference" choice.
Likelihood L(θ; x) = p(x | θ) The probability (density) of the observed data seen as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ (it need not integrate to 1 in θ). It tells which parameter values explain the data better. The statistical model of how the data are generated (binomial, normal, …). It is the same ingredient used by frequentist maximum likelihood.
Posterior π(θ | x) The distribution of θ after seeing the data: the complete result of the analysis, from which we obtain estimates (mean, mode: MAP), credible intervals and probabilities of hypotheses. Computed with Bayes' theorem.
Evidence p(x) The marginal likelihood: the probability of the data under the model averaged over the prior. A constant in θ; it is what is compared between different models (Bayes factors). Integral of likelihood × prior.

Why the updating is central

  • Probabilities for parameters. The posterior answers directly "what is the probability that θ is in [a, b] given what I saw?" (credible interval), something a frequentist confidence interval does not provide.
  • Sequential learning. The posterior after the first batch of data becomes the prior for the next one. Updating batch by batch gives exactly the same result as using all data at once. This makes Bayesian methods natural for streaming data and for accumulating threat intelligence.
  • Compromise between knowledge and data. The posterior is a weighted combination of the prior and the likelihood: with little data it leans on the prior; with a lot of data it is dominated by the data.
  • Prediction. The predictive probability of a new observation averages over the posterior, so it propagates the uncertainty on θ.

Example 1: Beta–Binomial (an unknown success probability)

Model

θ = probability that an attack attempt succeeds. Data: k successes in n independent attempts, so the likelihood is L(θ) ∝ θk(1 − θ)n−k. Prior: Beta(a, b), π(θ) ∝ θa−1(1 − θ)b−1 (it is a family of distributions on [0, 1]; its mean is a/(a + b)).

Posterior posterior ∝ θk(1 − θ)n−k · θa−1(1 − θ)b−1 = θa+k−1(1 − θ)b+n−k−1 ⇒ θ | data ~ Beta(a + k, b + n − k)

The posterior is in the same family as the prior (the prior is conjugate to the likelihood), so the update is just arithmetic: add the successes to a and the failures to b. The parameters a and b act as "pseudo-observations": a prior successes and b prior failures.

Posterior mean as a weighted average E[θ | data] = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w · (a/(a + b)) + (1 − w) · (k/n) w = (a + b)/(a + b + n)

11 Applications, advantages and limitations

Applications that use priors and likelihoods

Application Prior Likelihood Posterior / use
Spam filter (naive Bayes) fraction of spam in the mailbox P(words | spam) and P(words | legitimate), multiplied word by word assuming conditional independence P(spam | email): classify above a threshold
Intrusion detection base rate of attacks; threat intelligence on the host P(alert | attack), P(alert | normal) P(attack | alert): triage priority (Section 7)
Rate of rare events (breaches, failures) Gamma or Beta prior from similar systems Poisson or binomial counts, even zero events observed posterior credible bounds for the rate; with zero events the frequentist estimate is 0 while the Bayesian one is positive
A/B testing of controls Beta priors for the two success rates binomial counts P(rateB > rateA | data): a directly interpretable probability
Sequential monitoring yesterday's posterior today's batch of events updated risk estimate without reprocessing the history
Model comparison prior over models marginal likelihood p(x | model) posterior model probabilities, Bayes factors

Advantages and limitations

Advantages Limitations
Direct probability statements about parameters and hypotheses (credible intervals, P(H | data)). Subjectivity of the prior: different analysts may reach different conclusions, especially with little data.
Uses prior knowledge: previous studies and expert information are incorporated formally. Prior sensitivity: an unreasonable or dogmatic prior can dominate; needs sensitivity analysis and transparency.
Sequential updating: natural for streaming data; the order of the batches does not matter. Computation: apart from conjugate cases the posterior has no closed form and needs MCMC or variational methods (costly, with convergence diagnostics).
Works with small samples and rare events, where asymptotic frequentist approximations fail. Model dependence: the posterior is only as good as the likelihood; Bayes does not repair a misspecified model.
Propagates uncertainty in prediction, decision-making (expected loss) and hierarchical models. Improper or "non-informative" priors can give improper posteriors or depend on the parametrization.
Coherent: a single rule (Bayes' theorem) for all inference problems. Communication: results depend on the prior, which must be explained to non-experts.

12 Conclusions of pt2

1. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B) restricts the sample space; Bayes' theorem P(A | B) = P(B | A)P(A)/P(B) inverts the conditioning and shows that P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A) unless priors are equal. Ignoring the base rate leads to gross errors, as in medical screening and intrusion detection.

2. Prior, likelihood, posterior

The prior π(θ) encodes what is believed before the data; the likelihood L(θ; x) = p(x | θ) is the model's account of the data as a function of θ; the posterior π(θ | x) ∝ L(θ; x)π(θ) combines them. The posterior is the complete answer and becomes the prior for the next batch of data: this sequential updating is the heart of Bayesian inference.

3. Influence on conclusions

With little data the prior matters (a strong or wrong prior can dominate); with abundant data the likelihood dominates and posteriors under different priors agree, with width shrinking like 1/√n (the same √n of pt1). Good practice: state the prior, check the sensitivity of the conclusions to it, and remember that the posterior is only as good as the model.
