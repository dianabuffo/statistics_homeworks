Welford's Recurrences and Sampling Mean & Variance

Computing the mean and the variance of a stream of scores with a few lines of code, then checking on m simulated hackers that these estimates are centered on the true μ and σ², and how their spread shrinks as n grows.

pt1 pt2
1. Derive Welford's formulas
Starting from Knuth's update for the mean, we derive the update for the sum of squares and the variance. The code needs only 3 lines.
2. Estimate with m hackers
Each hacker gets n scores from 0 to 10. Welford gives the sampling mean and variance in a single pass, without storing the scores.
3. Compare with μ and σ²
The averages over the hackers land on the true values (unbiased estimators), and their spread shrinks like 1/√n.

What are we doing and what is the objective?

pt1 (pure mathematics + code): we want to compute the mean and the variance of a long list of numbers without keeping the list, updating the result every time a new number arrives. We derive the update rules (Welford's recurrences) and write the code.

pt2 (simulation): we extend Homework 3. Each of m hackers attacks n systems and gets, for every system, a score from 0 to 10 drawn from a fixed distribution with known mean μ and variance σ². Each hacker uses Welford's formulas to compute the sampling mean and the sampling variance of their own n scores. We then average these quantities over the m hackers and compare them with μ and σ².

The objective: to understand that (1) the sampling mean and the sampling variance are estimators that, on average, hit the true values exactly (unbiasedness); (2) each single hacker is still off by a random amount, and this error shrinks as n grows; (3) this is the foundation of inferential statistics, where from one sample we say something reliable about the unknown population.

Study guide: start here (the vocabulary, from zero)

Parameters versus estimates (the most important distinction)

  • Population (theoretical) values μ and σ²: they belong to the rule that generates the scores. They are fixed numbers, computed from the probability of each score. In a real attack you do not know them.
  • Sample values Mn and sn²: they are computed from the n scores you actually observed. They change from one hacker to the next because the scores are random. They are estimates of μ and σ².
  • Estimator: a recipe that turns the data into an estimate. "Add the scores and divide by n" is an estimator of μ.

Words you need

  • Sample mean Mn: (x1 + … + xn)/n. Same quantity as Sn/n of Homework 3.
  • SSn (sum of squares): the sum of the squared distances from the sample mean, Σ(xi − Mn)². It is the "raw material" of the variance.
  • Sample variance sn²: SSn/(n−1). It estimates σ². The version with n at the denominator, SSn/n, is also used but it underestimates σ² (explained in Section 9).
  • Unbiased: an estimator is unbiased if its average over many repetitions equals the true value. It does not say that one single estimate is exact.
  • Standard error: the standard deviation of an estimator across repetitions. For the sample mean it is σ/√n. It measures "how wrong a single estimate typically is".
  • Recurrence / online algorithm: a formula that updates the result using only the previous result and the new datum. It uses constant memory.
  • Monte Carlo simulation: the computer plays the experiment many times with random numbers and we look at the pile of outcomes.

Exercise 1: Welford's recurrence formulas

Derive the recurrences for the sum of squares and the variance, in the same way as Knuth's recurrence for the mean, and write the code.

Derive Welford's recurrence formulas for the sum of squares (SS) and the variance, following the same approach as Knuth's recurrence formula for the mean. Write the corresponding code. The core logic should require only about 3 lines.

1 Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Numbers x1, x2, … arrive one at a time. Let Mn be the mean of the first n of them:

Mn = (1/n) · (x1 + x2 + … + xn)

We want Mn from Mn−1 and the new number xn only.

Step 1: rewrite the sum

The first n−1 numbers add up to (n−1)·Mn−1 (this is just the definition of mean, read backwards). So:

x1 + … + xn = (n−1)·Mn−1 + xn
Step 2: divide by n and rearrange Mn = [(n−1)·Mn−1 + xn] / n
    = Mn−1 − Mn−1/n + xn/n
    = Mn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)/n
Knuth's recurrence. Call δn = xn − Mn−1 (how far the new number is from the old mean, the "surprise"). Then Mn = Mn−1 + δn / n with M0 = 0

In words: the new mean is the old mean plus a fraction 1/n of the surprise. Starting from M0 = 0, the first step gives M1 = 0 + (x1 − 0)/1 = x1, as it must.

2 Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

Define the sum of squared deviations from the current mean:

SSn = ∑i=1n (xi − Mn)²

The difficulty: when a new number arrives, the mean changes, so every old deviation changes too. We must show that this can still be fixed with one cheap correction. We use two facts from Section 1:

(a) Mn − Mn−1 = δn/n
(b) xn − Mn = δn − δn/n = (n−1)δn/n
Step 1: separate the new term SSn = ∑i=1n−1 (xi − Mn)² + (xn − Mn)²
Step 2: express the old terms with the old mean

For i < n, add and subtract Mn−1 and use (a):

xi − Mn = (xi − Mn−1) − (Mn − Mn−1) = (xi − Mn−1) − δn/n
Step 3: expand the square and sum over i < n ∑i<n (xi − Mn)² = ∑i<n (xi − Mn−1)² − 2(δn/n)∑i<n (xi − Mn−1) + (n−1)δn²/n²

The first sum is SSn−1. The middle sum is zero: deviations from the mean always add up to zero (∑i<n xi = (n−1)Mn−1). So the old terms contribute

SSn−1 + (n−1)δn²/n²
Step 4: the new term, with fact (b) (xn − Mn)² = (n−1)²δn²/n²
Step 5: add the two pieces SSn = SSn−1 + δn² · [(n−1) + (n−1)²]/n² = SSn−1 + δn² · (n−1)n/n² = SSn−1 + (n−1)δn²/n
Welford's recurrence for SS. Because (xn − Mn) = (n−1)δn/n, the last result can be written as a product of two "surprises", one measured with the old mean and one with the new mean: SSn = SSn−1 + (xn − Mn−1) · (xn − Mn) with SS0 = 0

3 The recurrence for the variance

From SSn we get the two usual variances:

sample variance (unbiased): sn² = SSn / (n−1) for n ≥ 2
population-style variance: vn = SSn / n
Recurrence for the sample variance sn²

Use SSn = SSn−1 + (n−1)δn²/n and the fact that SSn−1 = (n−2)·sn−1²:

(n−1)·sn² = (n−2)·sn−1² + (n−1)δn²/n

Divide by (n−1):

sn² = [(n−2)/(n−1)]·sn−1² + δn²/n for n ≥ 2
Recurrence for vn = SSn/n vn = [(n−1)/n]·vn−1 + (n−1)δn²/n²
Summary of what we derived. δn = xn − Mn−1
Mn = Mn−1 + δn/n (Knuth)
SSn = SSn−1 + δn · (xn − Mn) (Welford)
sn² = SSn/(n−1)

In practice the simplest strategy is to update only M and SS and divide at the end. That is what the code below does.

4 The code (3 lines of core logic)

def welford(data):
    mean = ss = 0.0
    for n, x in enumerate(data, start=1):
        d = x - mean            # 1) surprise w.r.t. the OLD mean   (delta_n)
        mean += d / n           # 2) Knuth: update the mean
        ss += d * (x - mean)    # 3) Welford: update SS (uses the NEW mean)
    return mean, ss / (n - 1)   # sample mean, unbiased sample variance

The three core lines are the ones numbered 1, 2, 3. Everything else is bookkeeping. Note that memory use is constant: the data are never stored, so data can be a generator that reads a file or a network stream.

Quick check against NumPy (the two results must be equal up to rounding):

import numpy as np
x = np.random.default_rng(0).integers(0, 11, 1000)      # 1000 scores in 0..10
print(welford(x))                                       # (mean, variance) with Welford
print(x.mean(), x.var(ddof=1))                          # reference values

12 Conclusion

Computing the summary of this run...

1. What do we conclude from the comparison with μ and σ²?

The average of the sampling means over the hackers coincides with μ, and the average of the sampling variances (with the n−1 divisor) coincides with σ², for every n. The small differences are simulation noise (they stay within a couple of Monte Carlo standard errors, see the z-scores). In statistical language: the sampling mean and the sampling variance are unbiased estimators of μ and σ². The version of the variance that divides by n is instead biased downwards by the factor (n−1)/n.

2. Why are these results useful in inferential statistics?

  • In real life there is one sample (one pen-test campaign), and μ and σ² are unknown. Inference means using Mn and sn² to say something about them. Unbiasedness guarantees that this procedure does not push the answer systematically too high or too low.
  • Knowing the spread of the estimator (SD(Mn) = σ/√n, estimated by sn/√n, the standard error) lets us build confidence intervals (Mn ± 2 sn/√n contains μ about 95% of the time, thanks to the CLT) and carry out hypothesis tests ("is the breach score of this defense really lower than 5?").
  • The Welford formulas make all this computable on a data stream, with constant memory and without numerical problems, which is what monitoring and detection systems actually need.
  • The simulation with m hackers is a laboratory device: we can check on many repetitions what theory claims about estimators, something impossible with a single real sample.

3. What happens to the variability when n increases?

The center of the estimators does not change (they are unbiased for every n), but their spread decreases like 1/√n: SD(Mn) = σ/√n, and SD(sn²) is proportional to 1/√n too (with a constant that depends on the fourth moment). The histograms of Section 8 get narrower, the trajectories of Section 7 funnel in, the log-log lines of Section 10 have slope −1/2. The estimators are consistent: they converge to the true values. And the price of precision is steep: four times more systems to halve the error.

Connection with Homework 3

The sampling mean Mn is exactly the "average score" Sn/n of Homework 3, so Var(Mn) = σ²/n, the Law of Large Numbers and the CLT apply to it directly. The new ingredient is that σ² is also estimated from the data, and that the estimator needs the n−1 correction to be unbiased.

Concept One-sentence version Where to see it
Knuth (mean) Mn = Mn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)/n: the new mean is the old one plus 1/n of the surprise. Section 1
Welford (SS) SSn = SSn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)(xn − Mn): old surprise times new surprise. Sections 2, 4
Variance sn² = SSn/(n−1), also sn² = [(n−2)/(n−1)]sn−1² + δn²/n. Section 3
Why Welford One pass, constant memory, and numerically stable (the naive formula suffers catastrophic cancellation). Section 5
Unbiasedness E[Mn] = μ and E[sn²] = σ² for every n; dividing SS by n gives σ²(n−1)/n. Section 9
Standard error SD(Mn) = σ/√n; the variance estimator also has spread ∝ 1/√n (with the fourth moment). Sections 8, 10
Why it matters With one real sample we estimate unknown parameters without systematic error and quantify the uncertainty (confidence intervals, tests). Section 12
