Stochastic Penetration Processes: Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem

A complete study of stochastic penetration across n systems and m attackers: trajectory behavior, variance scaling, the Weak Law of Large Numbers, and Central Limit Theorem convergence.

1. Simulate Penetration Trajectories
Modeling m independent attackers across n systems under Bernoulli breach and symmetric Rademacher walks.
2. Track Variance & Convergence
Proving the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) for average scores and discovering the invariant √n scaling.
3. Central Limit Theorem & Risk
Watching the Gaussian bell curve emerge by the CLT and analyzing aggregate security exposure for enterprise defense.

What are we doing and what is the objective?

What we are doing: We model a computer network penetration test where attackers sequentially attempt to compromise a series of n defensive systems. We simulate m independent attackers performing this test under two different scoring models:

  • Bernoulli Model: At each system, an attacker either breaches the target with score +1 (probability p) or the defense holds with score 0 (probability 1 − p). This represents cumulative breach counting.
  • Rademacher Model: At each system, an attacker gets +1 for an offensive breakthrough, or −1 when intercepted by security countermeasures (each with probability 1/2). This represents a symmetric, zero-mean confrontation.

Across these simulated runs, we track three mathematical quantities: the Total Score (Sₙ), the Average Score (Sₙ/n), and the Normalized Score (Sₙ/√n).

The Objective: To understand how random individual outcomes transform into predictable aggregate laws as the number of systems n grows. Specifically, we want to see: (1) why the total score spreads out unpredictably; (2) why the average score strictly converges to a single constant number by the Law of Large Numbers; (3) why dividing by √n is the unique scaling that stabilizes variance; and (4) why the standardized sum converges into a universal Gaussian bell curve by the Central Limit Theorem. Finally, we translate these mathematical findings into concrete cybersecurity decisions regarding defensive depth versus attacker volume.

1 Stochastic Modeling of the Penetration Process

Imagine an enterprise network protected by n defense checkpoints (firewalls, internal subnets, authentication gates). An attacker attempts to cross them one by one. At each checkpoint i, the interaction generates a penetration score Xi.

The increments X₁, X₂, …, Xn are modeled as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables:

  • Identically distributed: Every system in the chain enforces equivalent baseline security rules, so each step shares the same probability distribution.
  • Independent: What happens at system i does not change the defensive barrier of system i+1.
Model Support Probability Law Mean μ = E[Xi] Variance σ² = Var(Xi) Cybersecurity Meaning
Bernoulli Model {0, 1} P(Xᵢ = 1) = p
P(Xᵢ = 0) = 1 − p		 p p(1 − p) Breach Counting
Each system is either compromised (+1) or holds (0). The score never decreases. Over n systems, the expected total score climbs steadily as n · p.
Rademacher Model {−1, +1} P(Xᵢ = +1) = 1/2
P(Xᵢ = −1) = 1/2		 0 1 Symmetric Clash
Gains (+1) and active defensive blocks (−1) are equally likely. Because (+1)(0.5) + (−1)(0.5) = 0, defensive successes balance out attacker gains on average. The score wanders around zero without any upward or downward drift.

Comparing these two models allows us to isolate two different phenomena: Bernoulli introduces a non-zero mean (μ = p > 0), creating a steady upward drift; Rademacher is symmetric around zero (μ = 0), isolating the pure diffusion of random fluctuations without any drift.

2 The Three Quantities: Total, Average, and Normalized Score

When we sum n independent random steps, the sum grows, but so does its uncertainty. To understand how the process behaves as networks grow larger, we examine the sum at three different scales:

Quantity Formula Expected Value Theoretical Variance Asymptotic Behavior
Total Score Sₙ = ∑ᵢ₁ⁿ Xᵢ n · μ n · σ² = O(n) Because independent variances add up directly (Var = nσ²), the standard deviation grows as σ√n. The spread between lucky and unlucky attackers widens indefinitely.
Average Score Sₙ / n = (1/n) ∑ᵢ₁ⁿ Xᵢ μ σ² / n = O(1/n) Dividing by n scales variance by 1/n², giving Var = σ²/n. As n grows, the variance drops to 0. All attackers end up with the exact same average breach rate μ.
Normalized Score Sₙ / √n = (1/√n) ∑ᵢ₁ⁿ Xᵢ μ√n
(= 0 for Rademacher)		 σ² = O(1)
(Constant invariant)		 Dividing by √n scales variance by (1/√n)² = 1/n, giving Var = (nσ²)/n = σ². The variance does not depend on n. Fluctuations neither blow up to infinity nor collapse to zero.

This reveals why √n is the fundamental scale of random walks. If we divide by n, variance collapses to 0 and all fluctuation details disappear. If we do not divide at all, variance grows to infinity and trajectories blow up. Dividing by √n perfectly balances accumulation and scaling, keeping variance strictly constant at σ².

5 Weak Law of Large Numbers and Convergence in Probability

The Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) guarantees that as the number of systems n grows, the empirical average score Sₙ/n gets closer and closer to the theoretical expected score μ, and the probability of observing an outlier drops to zero:

For any positive tolerance margin ε > 0:
limn → ∞ P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) = 0

Why does this happen? (Chebyshev's Inequality):
Chebyshev's inequality states that for any random variable Y with finite variance, the probability of deviating from its expected value by more than ε is bounded by its variance: P(|Y − E[Y]| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Y) / ε².
Applying this to the average score Y = Sₙ/n, we know that E[Sₙ/n] = μ and Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n. Substituting gives:

P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ² / (n · ε²)

Because σ² and ε are fixed numbers, having n in the denominator forces the entire fraction to 0 as n → ∞. This proves that an attacker's average success rate will inevitably converge to μ.

6 Variance Scaling Under Different Normalizations

To verify how variance scales with n, we plot on a log-log scale. If a relationship follows a power law Var = c · nk, taking logarithms gives log₁₀(Var) = log₁₀(c) + k · log₁₀(n). On a log-log plot, this appears as a straight line whose slope k is exactly the scaling exponent:

Quantity Variance Formula Power Law Log-Log Slope (k) Meaning
Unnormalized Sₙ Var(Sₙ) = n · σ² ∝ n¹ +1.0 (Ascending line) Variance grows 10-fold whenever systems increase 10-fold.
Average Score Sₙ/n Var(Sₙ/n) = σ² / n ∝ n−¹ −1.0 (Descending line) Variance shrinks 10-fold whenever systems increase 10-fold.
Normalized Score Sₙ/√n Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ² ∝ n° = const 0.0 (Flat horizontal line) Variance is invariant to system size.

7 Central Limit Theorem and Gaussian Asymptotics

The Lindeberg-Levy Central Limit Theorem (CLT) states that the properly standardized sum of independent random variables always converges in distribution to the standard Gaussian bell curve N(0, 1):

As n → ∞,
Zₙ* = (Sₙ − nμ) / (σ√n) → N(0, 1)

Why Rademacher and Bernoulli behave differently:

  • Rademacher Case (μ = 0, σ = 1): Because the mean is already zero, the normalized score is already standardized: Sₙ/√n ≡ (Sₙ − n(0))/(1·√n) = Zₙ*. It converges directly to N(0, 1) without any shifting. In addition, because +1 and −1 are equally likely, the distribution has zero skewness (γ₁ = 0). By the Berry-Esseen theorem, convergence to the bell curve is very fast.
  • Bernoulli Case (μ = p > 0, σ = √(p(1−p))): Because each step has a positive expected value p, the raw sum Sₙ/√n has an expected value of p√n → ∞. To observe the Gaussian shape, we must subtract np and divide by √(np(1−p)). Furthermore, if p is far from 0.5 (e.g. p = 0.10), the initial distribution is skewed, requiring a larger n before the bell shape looks symmetric.
