Statistical Independence: Contingency Tables and the Chi-square Test

From a list of paired observations to a contingency table, from marginal and conditional frequencies to the frequencies expected under independence, and from the chi-square statistic to a decision: are the two variables independent or associated, and what does that (not) tell us about causes?

1. Build and describe the table
From raw pairs (X, F) to the contingency table, with marginal, conditional and joint frequencies computed by code.
2. Test independence
Expected frequencies under independence, chi-square statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, critical value and effect size.
3. Interpret with care
Independence in probability versus in statistics, what a small p-value does and does not say, association versus causality (Simpson's paradox).

What are we doing and what is the objective?

The assignment. Given a bivariate dataset (X, F) we must (1) programmatically construct the contingency table and compute marginal and conditional frequencies; (2) compute the frequencies expected under independence and the chi-square statistic (optionally the p-value); (3) recall the theory of independence in probability and in statistics, interpret the results, and briefly discuss why statistical association is not causality.

The objective: to understand that "independent" is a precise mathematical property (the joint distribution is the product of the marginals) that a finite sample can never satisfy exactly; that the chi-square test measures how far the observed table is from the table that independence would produce, compared with what random sampling alone could do; and that the answer concerns association, never directly cause and effect.

Study guide: start here (the vocabulary, from zero)

The data

  • Bivariate dataset (X, F): N observations, each one made of a pair (xk, fk): two things measured on the same individual (for instance the passenger class and whether the person survived).
  • Categorical variable: a variable whose values are labels (class 1/2/3, yes/no). A numeric variable with many values can be turned into categories by grouping (binning).
  • Contingency table: a table of counts nij = how many observations have X = i-th category and F = j-th category. Rows are the categories of X, columns those of F.

The frequencies

  • Joint frequency nij/N: the share of all observations in cell (i, j).
  • Marginal frequencies (the "margins" of the table): row totals ni· and column totals n·j, divided by N: the distribution of X alone and of F alone.
  • Conditional frequency nij/ni·: among the individuals with X = i, the share with F = j. It answers "within this group, how are the F values distributed?".

The test

  • Independence: knowing the value of one variable tells you nothing about the other. In a table: all the rows have the same conditional distribution.
  • Expected frequency Eij: the count that cell (i, j) would have if independence held exactly.
  • Chi-square statistic χ²: one number that measures the total gap between observed and expected counts.
  • Null hypothesis H0: "X and F are independent in the population". p-value: the probability, if H0 were true, of getting a table at least as far from independence as the observed one. Significance level α: the threshold (usually 0.05) below which we say "too unlikely, we reject H0".
  • Degrees of freedom (df): the number of cells that are free to vary once the margins are fixed: (r − 1)(c − 1).
  • Cramér's V: a number between 0 and 1 telling how strong the association is (the p-value tells how surprising it is: a different thing).
  • Confounder: a third variable that influences both X and F and creates (or hides) an association between them.
How to study this page. Start with the Titanic data (the default). Follow the numbers from the raw pairs down to the decision, then change the dataset to a synthetic one that is independent by construction and see what a "no association" result looks like. Finally read Section 9 on causality.

1 Independence: probability versus statistics

In probability (a property of a model)

Two events

A and B are independent when

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) equivalently (if P(B) > 0): P(A | B) = P(A)

Learning that B happened does not change the probability of A.

Two discrete random variables X and F

They are independent when the joint probability is the product of the marginal probabilities, for every pair of values:

P(X = x, F = y) = P(X = x) · P(F = y) for all x, y equivalently P(F = y | X = x) = P(F = y) for every x

For continuous variables the joint density factorizes, f(x, y) = fX(x) fF(y), and so does the joint CDF.

Consequences and warnings.

  • Independence implies zero covariance (E[XF] = E[X]E[F]), but not conversely: if X is uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and F = X², then Cov(X, F) = 0 yet F is completely determined by X.
  • Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive: if A and B cannot occur together (P(A ∩ B) = 0) and both have positive probability, then P(A ∩ B) ≠ P(A)P(B), so they are strongly dependent.

In statistics (a property of data, and an hypothesis about a population)

With N observations we have relative frequencies instead of probabilities: the joint frequency fij = nij/N and the marginals fi· = ni·/N, f·j = n·j/N. The data are empirically independent when

fij = fi· · f·j for all i, j ⇔ nij = ni··n·j/N = Eij ⇔ all rows have the same conditional distribution

In a real sample this never holds exactly, even when the variables are truly independent in the population, because of sampling variability (the same reason why 10 coin tosses are rarely 5 heads and 5 tails). So we do not ask "is the table exactly independent?" but "is the observed gap from independence larger than what chance alone would normally produce?". That is a hypothesis test:

H0: X and F are independent in the population. H1: they are not (dependent / associated).
Pearson's statistic: χ² = ∑i,j (Oij − Eij)² / Eij, which under H0 follows approximately a chi-square distribution with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom.

4 Expected frequencies under independence

The formula

If X and F were independent, the share of observations in cell (i, j) would be the product of the shares of its row and its column, and the expected count is that share times N:

Eij = N · fi· · f·j = N · (ni·/N) · (n·j/N) = ni· · n·j / N

The expected table has exactly the same margins as the observed one, but all its rows are proportional to each other: it is the closest "perfectly independent" table that shares the observed margins.

Distribution of F inside each category of X

Mosaic plot, shaded by residual

What it means: left, every bar is a category of X and its colored slices are the conditional distribution of F (they sum to 100%); the bar "All" is the marginal distribution of F. If X and F were independent, every bar would look like the bar "All". Right, the width of each column is proportional to the size of the X category, the height of each tile to the conditional share of F, so the area of a tile is proportional to the count. Blue tiles have more observations than expected under independence, red tiles fewer (darker = larger residual). Under independence all the lines separating the tiles would line up horizontally across the columns.

9 Association is not causation: confounders and Simpson's paradox

The chi-square test, like any measure of association, answers only "are X and F statistically linked?". It does not say why. Here is a famous real example: two treatments for kidney stones (A and B) and their success rates, aggregated and by stone size (Charig et al., 1986).

What it means: inside each stone size, treatment A has the higher success rate. In the aggregated data, treatment B looks better. The reversal happens because the treatment was not assigned at random: A was mostly used on large stones (263 of its 350 cases), which are harder to treat, and B mostly on small ones. Stone size is a confounder: it influences both which treatment is chosen and the outcome.

1. Statistical independence

Two variables are independent when the joint distribution is the product of the marginals, equivalently when all conditional distributions coincide with the marginal one. In a sample, this corresponds to observed counts equal to ni·n·j/N. Because of sampling variability this never holds exactly, so we use the χ² test: if the statistic is large compared with a χ² distribution with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom (small p-value), we reject independence; otherwise we do not.

2. Interpretation of the result

A small p-value is evidence of a statistical association; the residuals say where it lives and Cramér's V says how strong it is. A large p-value means that the data are compatible with independence, not that independence is proven. Both depend on N, so the sample size must always be reported.

3. Association versus causality

An association can arise from a causal link in either direction, from a confounding variable (Simpson's paradox shows how it can even reverse the sign of the effect) or from chance. The test cannot tell these apart. Causal conclusions require experimental design (randomization) or a causal model, not only a contingency table.
